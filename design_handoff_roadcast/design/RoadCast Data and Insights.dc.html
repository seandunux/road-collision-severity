How often is the outcome serious or fatal?

About 1 in 4 recorded collisions had a serious or fatal outcome.

This imbalance is why the model is class-weighted during training and its output is calibrated afterwards.

Severity rate by speed limit

The recorded rate rises with the speed limit, peaking at 33.8% on 60 mph roads.

70 mph roads, mostly motorways and dual carriageways, sit lower at 24.4%. This is an association in recorded collisions, not evidence that a speed limit alone changes outcomes.

Severity rate by road type

Single carriageways have the highest recorded rate, at 25.0%.

Collisions with no recorded road type have the lowest rate (8.6%). That more likely reflects less detailed reporting than safer roads.

Urban and rural areas

Rural collisions were recorded as serious or fatal at 28.8%, compared with 20.7% in urban areas.

Rural areas account for about a third of recorded collisions.

Lighting conditions

Darkness without street lighting has the highest recorded rate, at 34.2%.

Unlit roads are more common in rural, higher-speed settings, so this likely overlaps with the patterns above rather than being a separate effect.

Source: Department for Transport, STATS19 road collision data, Great Britain, 2020–2024.

How the model works

Understand which recorded features have the greatest influence on the model’s estimates.

Key finding

Road structure, speed, and rural location influence the model more strongly than weather.

No weather category appears among the 20 most influential features.

Feature importance

Single-carriageway roads, the speed limit and rural location are the three strongest influences.

Each value is the average size of a feature’s influence on the model output across 5,000 evaluation collisions, regardless of direction.

Direction of effect

Importance says how much a feature matters. This chart shows which way it pushes.

Each dot is one evaluation collision. Its horizontal position is how far that feature moved the estimate; its shade is the feature’s value in that collision.

Reading one feature: speed limit

Dark dots (high speed limits) sit right of the centre line, so higher recorded limits pushed estimates higher. Light dots (20–30 mph) sit to the left and pushed estimates lower.

SHAP shows how recorded features influenced this model’s output across the evaluation sample. It explains model behaviour, not real-world causation. Strong effects for missing or unknown categories may indicate patterns in data collection rather than genuine safety effects.

What calibrated means

When the model predicts approximately 30%, about 30% of comparable cases in the evaluation data had a serious or fatal outcome.

Why the alert threshold is 22.5%

Chosen to catch as many serious or fatal collisions as possible while keeping precision at or above 30% on the evaluation set. It currently sits just below the 23.3% baseline, so an estimate can be above the threshold while still close to the average collision.

About the data

What the data represents

This model was trained on police-reported STATS19 collision records from Great Britain. It describes patterns in recorded collisions and should not be interpreted as representing every collision that occurred.

Recording effects

Most inputs describe recorded road, environmental, and time conditions. Missing or unknown values can also reflect how information was collected at the scene.

How to interpret the result

The percentage is a calibrated model estimate for collisions with similar recorded characteristics. It is intended for exploration and screening, not as a prediction of what will happen in an individual event.

Association, not causation

The explanation shows which recorded features influenced the model. It does not prove that those conditions caused a serious or fatal outcome.
